Brain Eating Amoeba North Carolina: Teen Critically Ill After Rare Infection

A teenager in central North Carolina is critically ill after developing a rare infection caused by Naegleria fowleri, commonly described as the “brain-eating amoeba.” North Carolina health officials said the infection can occur when contaminated warm freshwater enters the nose during swimming or other water activities.

Naegleria fowleri is a microscopic organism that can live naturally in warm freshwater such as lakes, rivers and ponds. The infection is extremely rare, but when it occurs it can cause primary amebic meningoencephalitis, or PAM, a serious infection that affects the brain.

According to North Carolina health officials, the amoeba does not cause infection when contaminated water is swallowed. The main concern is when water containing the organism is forced up the nose, allowing the amoeba to travel toward the brain. The infection is not spread from person to person.

Early symptoms of PAM can include severe headache, fever, nausea and vomiting. The illness can then progress rapidly, with symptoms such as a stiff neck, seizures, confusion and coma. Because the disease can progress quickly, medical attention is important when concerning symptoms occur after exposure to warm freshwater.

Health officials emphasize that the risk of contracting Naegleria fowleri is extremely low. However, people can reduce their exposure by avoiding activities that force warm freshwater into the nose. Swimmers can keep their heads above water, use nose clips or hold their noses when going underwater.

Extra caution is recommended during prolonged periods of hot weather, when freshwater temperatures rise and water levels fall. Avoiding activities that disturb sediment in shallow, warm freshwater may also reduce exposure. Properly maintained and chlorinated swimming pools are not considered a typical environment for this amoeba.

The North Carolina case is a reminder that Naegleria fowleri can occur in freshwater environments in the state. Health officials have previously reported infections in North Carolina, although cases remain exceptionally uncommon. People should not avoid all freshwater recreation because of the extremely low risk, but sensible precautions can help reduce exposure.

NOAA's Climate Prediction Center issued an El Nino Advisory in August 2026 and reported that El Nino was strengthening. NOAA said there is a greater than 90 percent chance of a very strong El Nino during the Northern Hemisphere fall and winter of 2026-2027. The agency also noted that a strong El Nino does not guarantee identical weather impacts everywhere in the United States.

That distinction is important when looking at an El Nino snow map. El Nino is a large-scale climate pattern, not a storm forecast. It can influence the position of the jet stream and the movement of storms, but individual snowstorms depend on many other factors. Temperature, storm track, moisture, the Arctic Oscillation and other atmospheric patterns can determine whether a particular storm produces rain, sleet, freezing rain or heavy snow.

The Farmers Almanac winter 2026-2027 forecast is calling for a season of sharp contrasts. Its outlook describes an active southern storm track, quick bursts of snow and bitter cold across parts of the North, and a complicated mixture of rain, sleet, ice and heavy wet snow in the Northeast.

The southern United States could be one of the wetter parts of the country during the coming winter. The Farmers Almanac expects an active storm track extending from Southern California and the Desert Southwest through Texas, the Gulf Coast and the Southeast. When moisture-rich storms meet colder air, some areas could experience winter precipitation, including sleet, freezing rain and heavy wet snow.

The southern Rockies may have some of the better opportunities for significant mountain snowfall. Elevation is extremely important in this region because colder temperatures at higher elevations allow precipitation to fall and accumulate as snow even when lower elevations are experiencing rain.

Texas could also attract attention during the winter because of the combination of moisture and occasional cold air. However, a wet winter does not automatically mean a snowy winter. Many storms across Texas can produce heavy rain when temperatures are above freezing. Snow or ice becomes more likely when Arctic or continental cold air reaches the region at the same time that a storm is producing precipitation.

The Southeast could also experience frequent storm systems. Northern parts of the region and higher elevations have a better chance of seeing wintry precipitation than areas along the immediate Gulf Coast or Florida. A small change in temperature can make the difference between cold rain, sleet, freezing rain and snow.

The Northeast is expected to have one of the more complicated winter patterns. Coastal areas may see rain or mixed precipitation while locations farther inland could experience accumulating snow. January and February could be particularly important for periods of heavy wet snow, although exact storm tracks cannot be predicted this far in advance.

A strong El Nino does not necessarily mean that the Northeast will have nonstop snow. In fact, El Nino winters can sometimes produce warmer conditions in parts of the northern United States. However, individual cold-air outbreaks can still occur. If a strong storm develops while cold air is established near the surface, the result can be a significant snowstorm.

The Great Lakes region is another area to watch. Lake-effect snow can produce very heavy snowfall in narrow bands when cold air moves across relatively warmer lake water. The exact location of these snow bands depends on wind direction, temperature differences and the shape of the storm system.

The northern Plains and Upper Midwest could experience rapid temperature changes. The Farmers Almanac expects quick cold snaps, fast-moving snow systems and periods of strong wind, but these wintry periods may be interrupted by milder weather. This means the winter may alternate between cold and relatively mild conditions rather than remaining continuously frozen.

The Pacific Northwest has a different outlook. El Nino often favors a drier and warmer pattern across parts of the Pacific Northwest, although individual storms can still produce mountain snow. Lower elevations may have less dependable snow while higher mountain locations can continue to receive substantial snowfall when colder systems arrive.

California and the Southwest could see increased precipitation during an El Nino winter. Southern California and the Desert Southwest can become wetter when the subtropical jet stream becomes more active. Heavy rain is possible, while higher elevations can receive snow. Mountain snowpack can therefore be very different from conditions in nearby lower-elevation communities.

The phrase El Nino heavy snow can sometimes be misleading because El Nino does not simply make the entire United States snowier. Instead, it shifts the odds for different types of weather in different parts of the country. The southern storm track can become more active, while parts of the northern tier can experience warmer average conditions with occasional strong cold outbreaks.

The Farmers Almanac winter weather map is useful for understanding the broad seasonal outlook, but it should not be treated as a precise daily snow prediction. Long-range forecasts describe expected patterns months in advance. A local snowstorm can only be forecast with much greater confidence as the event gets closer.

Another important factor is the Arctic Oscillation. During a negative Arctic Oscillation phase, colder Arctic air can move farther south into the United States. This can temporarily overcome a warmer seasonal pattern and create major winter storms. This is one reason why a relatively mild seasonal forecast can still include episodes of heavy snow and dangerous cold.

The same principle applies to the Northeast. A mild period can quickly be followed by a snowstorm if the storm track changes and cold air becomes established. Coastal storms are especially sensitive to their exact path. A shift of only a small distance can change the precipitation type for millions of people.

In the South, heavy rain may be a greater concern than snow during many storms.

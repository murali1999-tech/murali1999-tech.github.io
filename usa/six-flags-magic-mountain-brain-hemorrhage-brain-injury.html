Six Flags Magic Mountain Brain Hemorrhage and Roller Coaster Injury

Six Flags Magic Mountain in Valencia, California, has recently been in the news because its X2 roller coaster has remained closed while state safety officials investigate an incident involving a rider. The situation has also renewed attention on earlier cases involving serious injuries and deaths associated with roller coasters at the park.

The current attention is focused on X2, one of the most recognizable and extreme attractions at Six Flags Magic Mountain. According to a Los Angeles Times report published in August 2026, the ride was closed after an incident on July 5 in which a person was reportedly unconscious and transported to a hospital. California's workplace safety agency, Cal/OSHA, opened an inspection into the reported incident.

The exact circumstances surrounding the July 5 incident have not been publicly established. Officials have not confirmed whether the person lost consciousness while riding X2 or at another point during the visit. Because the investigation remains ongoing, it is important not to assume that the incident was caused by a mechanical problem or that a particular injury occurred until authorities release more information.

The X2 attraction has been at Magic Mountain since 2002, when it originally opened as X. The ride reaches speeds of approximately 76 mph and is known for its unusual seating arrangement and intense movements. Unlike a traditional coaster, the seats rotate independently as the train travels through the track, creating a very different experience for riders.

The current closure has also brought renewed attention to the death of Christopher Hawley, a 22-year-old man who died in June 2022 after riding X2. His family later filed a wrongful-death lawsuit alleging that the ride caused a severe brain injury. The allegations in the lawsuit have been disputed by Six Flags, and the case remains a legal matter rather than a final finding that the ride caused his death.

According to reporting on the lawsuit, Hawley became unwell after getting off X2, complained of head pain and later collapsed. Medical treatment revealed severe brain bleeding. His family alleges that the ride was responsible for the injury. The lawsuit describes the coaster as excessively rough and alleges that the ride's movement and sudden stop contributed to the injury.

The Los Angeles Times reported that Hawley died the following day and that his family sued Six Flags. The lawsuit is separate from the current 2026 investigation into the July 5 incident. Keeping those events separate is important because the newer investigation has not publicly established the cause of the rider's condition.

The recent investigation is not the first time that Six Flags Magic Mountain has faced questions about rider injuries. The park has a long history of incidents involving guests and employees, as is the case with many large amusement parks. However, serious medical incidents are understandably subject to greater public attention.

Another widely reported case involved a woman named Pearl Santos, who died after riding the Goliath roller coaster in 2001. The ride was known for its speed and large drop. According to the Los Angeles County coroner's findings reported at the time, Santos suffered bleeding around the brain stem and a ruptured cerebral artery. Officials said an aneurysm may have been a preexisting condition, while also stating that the ride caused the fatal injuries.

The Goliath case is more than two decades old and should not be confused with the current X2 investigation. It is nevertheless part of the history that people often encounter when searching for Six Flags Magic Mountain brain hemorrhage or Six Flags roller coaster brain injury.

There have also been legal cases involving alleged concussions and other injuries at Magic Mountain. In one case, Talia Wise sued Six Flags, claiming that a roller coaster caused her concussion. Six Flags denied that the ride caused her brain injury, and the 2023 trial ended with a verdict for the defendant.

These cases demonstrate why reports about roller coaster injuries need to be read carefully. A lawsuit contains allegations made by one side, while the defendant may dispute those allegations. An investigation can also remain unresolved for months. A report that someone experienced a medical emergency at a theme park does not by itself establish that the ride caused the condition.

Medical researchers have also studied head movement during roller coaster rides. Research published through the National Institutes of Health examined head motions during roller coaster riding and compared them with other activities. The researchers found that the head motions measured during the rides studied were relatively mild and concluded that the risk of traumatic brain injury from those head motions appeared extremely low.

That research does not mean injuries are impossible. Individual rides, rider conditions, existing medical problems, impacts and other circumstances can differ. It also does not determine the cause of any particular incident at Six Flags Magic Mountain. Individual cases must be evaluated using their own medical and investigative evidence.

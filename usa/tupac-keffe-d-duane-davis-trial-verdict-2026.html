Tupac Trial Verdict: Keffe D Davis Found Guilty in Tupac Shakur Murder

Duane “Keffe D” Davis has been found guilty in the murder of Tupac Shakur, bringing a major development to one of the most closely followed cases in music history. A Las Vegas jury convicted Davis on August 31, 2026, after less than three hours of deliberations.

Davis, whose full name is Duane Keith Davis, was convicted of murder with the use of a deadly weapon and the intent to promote, further or assist a criminal gang. The verdict makes Davis the first person to be convicted in connection with Tupac Shakur's 1996 killing.

The case dates back almost 30 years. Tupac was shot in a drive-by shooting in Las Vegas on September 7, 1996, while riding in a BMW driven by Marion “Suge” Knight. Tupac was struck by four bullets and died six days later, on September 13, 1996, at the age of 25.

Who killed Tupac has been one of the biggest unanswered questions in hip-hop for decades. Prosecutors in the Davis case did not argue that Davis personally pulled the trigger. Instead, they argued that he organized the attack, obtained the firearm and passed it to people inside the white Cadillac involved in the shooting.

Prosecutors alleged that the shooting was retaliation following an earlier confrontation at the MGM Grand after the Mike Tyson-Bruce Seldon boxing match. Tupac and members of his entourage had been involved in an assault on Orlando “Baby Lane” Anderson, Davis' nephew.

According to the prosecution, the confrontation became the reason for the later attack. Prosecutors argued that Davis and others went looking for Tupac and Death Row Records co-founder Suge Knight. Later that night, the white Cadillac pulled alongside Knight's BMW at a Las Vegas intersection and gunfire erupted.

Davis had long been associated with the events surrounding the shooting, but he was not arrested until September 2023. His arrest represented a dramatic development in a case that had remained officially unsolved for many years.

One of the most important parts of the prosecution's case was Davis' own statements. Investigators had questioned him in previous years, and he also discussed Tupac's death in interviews, documentaries and his 2019 memoir, “Compton Street Legend.” Prosecutors used those statements to argue that Davis had repeatedly described his involvement in the events leading to the shooting.

A 2008 police interview was also admitted as evidence after a judge ruled that prosecutors could use the recording at trial. The prosecution argued that Davis' statements placed him inside the vehicle and showed his knowledge of the shooting.

The defense presented a different explanation. Davis' lawyers argued that his public statements were exaggerated stories designed to make money and increase his reputation. His attorney also questioned whether prosecutors had independent evidence placing Davis in Las Vegas and inside the Cadillac on the night of September 7, 1996.

Davis pleaded not guilty. He did not testify during the trial. The jury ultimately rejected the defense argument and returned a guilty verdict after approximately three hours of deliberation.

The trial lasted for weeks and included testimony from dozens of witnesses. Prosecutors presented evidence concerning the history between the rival groups, the events at the MGM Grand, Davis' statements and the circumstances surrounding the shooting.

The jury heard testimony from 24 prosecution witnesses and three defense witnesses. Recordings and previous statements made by Davis were also presented to jurors as prosecutors attempted to establish that he played an organizing role in the killing.

The prosecution did not need to prove that Davis was the person who fired the fatal shots. Their argument was that Davis could be held responsible for the murder because he helped orchestrate the attack. Nevada law allows people who intentionally participate in or help arrange a murder to face murder liability even if they do not personally fire the weapon.

The identity of the person who fired the fatal shots was not established at trial. Prosecutors had previously identified Orlando Anderson as a possible shooter, but Anderson was never charged in Tupac's death and died in 1998 in an unrelated shooting.

Suge Knight was also a central figure in the events of that night. Knight was driving the BMW in which Tupac was riding when the shooting occurred. Knight was wounded but survived. Tupac suffered multiple gunshot wounds and died six days later.

The murder of Tupac became one of the defining mysteries of the East Coast-West Coast hip-hop era. Tupac was one of the biggest stars in music at the time, and his death was followed by widespread speculation about gang rivalries, Death Row Records, Bad Boy Records and the broader tensions surrounding the rap industry.

The killing of The Notorious B.I.G., also known as Biggie, months later created another major unsolved hip-hop murder. The two cases have often been discussed together because of the rivalry between artists and groups associated with the East Coast and West Coast rap scenes. However, the cases are separate investigations.

The conviction of Davis does not mean that every question surrounding Tupac's death has been answered. It establishes Davis' criminal responsibility under the verdict reached by the Las Vegas jury, but questions remain about the exact roles of the other people who were in the Cadillac and who fired the shots.

Davis now faces the possibility of a life sentence. Sentencing will take place separately from the verdict. His defense has indicated that he plans to appeal the conviction.

The verdict was emotional for people who had followed the case for decades. Members of Tupac's family attended portions of the proceedings, and the trial brought renewed attention to the rapper's legacy and the circumstances surrounding his death.

Tupac Shakur was born on June 16, 1971, in New York City. He became a successful rapper, actor, poet and activist before his death at 25. His albums and songs continued to influence hip-hop long after his death, and he was inducted into the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame in 2017.

For nearly three decades, the question “who killed Tupac?” remained one of the most recognizable mysteries in popular culture. The 2026 conviction of Duane “Keffe D” Davis represents the first criminal conviction directly connected to the murder.

The verdict also changes the legal history of the case. For years, there had been no defendant facing a murder conviction for Tupac's death. Davis' prosecution eventually moved the investigation from a decades-old cold case into a courtroom, where jurors were asked to evaluate evidence that included Davis' own words.

The case now moves into its sentencing and appeals phase. The final outcome will depend on the court's sentencing decision and any future legal challenges brought by Davis' defense team.

For Tupac's family, fans and the wider hip-hop community, the guilty verdict represents a significant moment after nearly 30 years of uncertainty. It does not erase the unanswered questions surrounding the shooting, but it does establish that a Nevada jury found Davis criminally responsible for the murder.

The key facts are clear: Tupac Shakur was shot in Las Vegas on September 7, 1996, and died six days later. Duane “Keffe D” Davis was arrested in 2023, went on trial in 2026, and was found guilty on August 31, 2026. Prosecutors said he orchestrated the attack but did not accuse him of being the shooter. Davis pleaded not guilty and plans to appeal.

The Tupac trial has therefore reached a historic point, but the legal process is not necessarily finished. Sentencing and the expected appeal will determine the next stage of the case involving Duane Keith “Keffe D” Davis.

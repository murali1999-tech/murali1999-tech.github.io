Gloria Steinem dies at 92: pioneering feminist and Ms. magazine co-founder leaves lasting legacy

Gloria Steinem, the journalist, writer and activist who became one of the most recognisable figures of the modern women's movement in the United States, has died at the age of 92. Her death was announced on September 3, 2026, after she died at her home in New York City on September 2. Her foundation did not disclose a cause of death. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

For more than five decades, Steinem was closely associated with the campaign for gender equality. She used journalism, public speaking, organising and publishing to bring women's rights issues into mainstream American political and social debate. Her influence reached well beyond the United States, and she became an internationally recognised symbol of second-wave feminism.

Steinem was born Gloria Marie Steinem on March 25, 1934, in Toledo, Ohio. She attended Smith College and graduated in 1956. After college, she received a Chester Bowles Fellowship that allowed her to spend two years in India. That experience had an important influence on her thinking about grassroots organising and social activism. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

She began her professional life as a journalist in New York at a time when women faced significant barriers in newsrooms. Steinem initially worked on assignments that were often directed toward women, but she wanted to report on politics and broader social issues. Her determination eventually helped her establish herself as a serious political writer.

One of the assignments that brought Steinem national attention came in 1963, when she went undercover at the Playboy Club for Show magazine. Her reporting examined the working conditions experienced by the women employed as Playboy Bunnies. The assignment became an important early example of her willingness to investigate subjects involving women's experiences and workplace inequality. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

Steinem later became involved with New York magazine, helping to establish the publication in 1968. She worked as a political columnist and feature writer and increasingly became involved in the emerging women's liberation movement.

A significant turning point came in 1969 when Steinem spoke publicly at an abortion-rights event in New York. Her involvement with the women's movement grew rapidly afterward, and she became a prominent speaker at demonstrations, conferences and political events.

Her name became particularly connected with Ms. magazine. Steinem worked with Patricia Carbine, Letty Cottin Pogrebin and other journalists and activists to establish a publication that would give women's issues a dedicated national platform.

Ms. first appeared as an insert in New York magazine in December 1971. It became an independent magazine in 1972. Steinem remained an editor for approximately 15 years and continued to have an association with the publication afterward. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

The magazine addressed subjects that were often ignored or treated differently by mainstream publications, including reproductive rights, domestic violence, sexual harassment, political equality and discrimination. Its arrival represented an important change in American magazine publishing and helped create a national forum for discussions about women's lives.

Steinem's activism went beyond journalism. In 1972, she helped establish the National Women's Political Caucus alongside other prominent feminists and political figures. The organisation worked to increase women's participation in political life and support women seeking elected and appointed positions.

She also helped establish several other organisations during her career, including the Women's Action Alliance, Voters for Choice, the Ms. Foundation for Women and the Women's Media Center. Her official biography lists her involvement in a wide range of organisations focused on equality, media representation and social change. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

Steinem was also a prolific writer. Her books included Outrageous Acts and Everyday Rebellions, Revolution from Within, Moving Beyond Words, Marilyn: Norma Jean and My Life on the Road. Her writing often connected personal experiences with wider questions about equality, power and social expectations.

Her career was not without controversy or criticism. Like many major public figures, Steinem faced disagreements both from opponents of feminism and from people within the women's movement. Questions were raised over her political positions, her public image and some of her earlier professional associations. She nevertheless remained one of the movement's most visible and influential public figures.

Steinem received numerous awards during her lifetime. Among the most significant was the Presidential Medal of Freedom, which she received from President Barack Obama in 2013. The award recognised her decades of work advocating for women's equality and social justice.

Her connection with India was another notable part of her early life. Her two years in the country exposed her to forms of grassroots activism that later influenced her approach to organising. Steinem's experiences there became part of the story of how a young journalist developed into an international activist. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}

Steinem also continued writing and speaking about women's rights well into later life. Rather than treating feminism as a subject belonging only to one generation, she repeatedly argued that questions involving equality, opportunity and representation remained relevant.

Her death closes the life of a woman who played a central role in shaping the public conversation around women's rights in the United States. Supporters and fellow activists have remembered her not only as a writer and speaker but also as an organiser who helped build institutions that continued working after individual campaigns had ended.

Steinem's legacy is particularly visible through Ms. magazine and the organisations she helped create. Her work helped bring issues that were once discussed privately into public debate. The influence of those discussions can be seen in journalism, politics, workplaces, education and popular culture.

Her death has already prompted tributes from prominent figures who knew and worked with her. Hillary Clinton, a longtime friend, described Steinem as an important force for women's rights and recalled a recent visit with her. Clinton said Steinem's example continued to demonstrate the importance of the fight for equality. :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6}

Steinem's life also demonstrated how journalism could become a route into activism. She began as a reporter seeking opportunities in a male-dominated industry and eventually became a leading public voice on questions of equality. Her career crossed journalism, publishing, politics and grassroots organising.

At 92, Gloria Steinem leaves behind a body of work that spans decades of American social change. Her name will remain closely associated with the second-wave feminist movement, Ms. magazine and the broader struggle for women's equality. Her influence continues through the publications, organisations and generations of activists shaped by her work.

The story of Gloria Steinem is therefore larger than a single political campaign or publication. It is the story of a journalist who became an organiser, a publisher who created a national platform, and an activist whose work helped change how women's rights were discussed in American public life.

Excessive Heat and 35,000 Deaths in Europe: What the Numbers Mean

Extreme heat has become one of Europe's most serious weather and public health concerns. During periods of unusually high temperatures, hospitals can see more patients suffering from heat exhaustion, dehydration and other conditions that become more dangerous when the body is unable to cool itself properly. Recent research has estimated that tens of thousands of deaths across Europe can be associated with extreme summer temperatures.

The figure of around 35,000 deaths has attracted attention because it shows the scale of the problem beyond the dramatic images of heatwaves. Heat does not normally appear as the direct cause of death in every case. Instead, researchers examine excess mortality, comparing the number of deaths during unusually hot periods with the number that would normally be expected under cooler conditions.

Older people are particularly vulnerable. The risk can also be higher for people with existing heart or lung problems, those who live alone and people who work outdoors. Very young children can also have difficulty regulating their body temperature during severe heat.

Heat can place additional stress on the cardiovascular system. The body sends more blood toward the skin in an effort to release heat, while sweating causes the loss of water and minerals. During prolonged hot weather, these effects can become dangerous, especially when nighttime temperatures remain high and the body does not get enough opportunity to recover.

European cities can face an additional problem because buildings, roads and other structures absorb heat during the day and release it slowly after sunset. This can create an urban heat island effect, with some densely developed areas remaining warmer than nearby rural locations.

Scientists have increasingly examined the connection between extreme heat and climate change. A warmer climate does not mean that every day will be unusually hot, but it increases the likelihood and intensity of certain heat extremes. This has made preparation increasingly important for governments, cities and public health agencies.

Simple measures can reduce the danger. Health authorities generally advise people to drink enough fluids, avoid strenuous activity during the hottest part of the day, stay in cooler indoor spaces when possible and check on elderly relatives, neighbors and other people who may be vulnerable.

Public cooling centers, heat warnings and better access to green spaces can also help communities deal with extreme temperatures. Improving building design and reducing the amount of heat absorbed by urban surfaces are longer-term measures that can make cities safer during future heatwaves.

The estimate of roughly 35,000 heat-related deaths should therefore be understood as a warning about the health consequences of extreme temperatures rather than as a simple count of people who died directly from heatstroke. Researchers use statistical methods to estimate the additional deaths associated with periods of unusually high temperatures.

As European summers become a growing focus of climate and health research, heat preparedness is likely to remain an important issue. Extreme heat can arrive quickly, but its health effects can continue long after temperatures begin to fall. Better warnings, community support and preparation can help reduce the human cost of Europe's increasingly severe heat events.

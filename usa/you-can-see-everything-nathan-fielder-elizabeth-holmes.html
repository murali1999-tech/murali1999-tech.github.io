You Can See Everything: Nathan Fielder's Elizabeth Holmes Documentary

You Can See Everything is one of the most unexpected documentary projects of 2026. The A24 film brings together comedian and filmmaker Nathan Fielder, documentary filmmaker Lance Oppenheim and former Theranos founder Elizabeth Holmes. The project had been surrounded by secrecy and speculation for years before A24 revealed the movie following its surprise world premiere at the Telluride Film Festival in September 2026.

The documentary focuses on Holmes, the controversial Silicon Valley entrepreneur whose company Theranos promised to transform blood testing. Rather than presenting a conventional historical documentary assembled entirely from interviews and archival material, You Can See Everything takes viewers directly into an unusual period of Holmes's life. According to the film's official description, Holmes invited a skeptical film crew to document her life 34 days before she was sent to prison.

That starting point immediately makes the movie different from many documentaries about Theranos. Holmes was already one of the most recognizable figures in the technology scandal when the cameras began following her. Her rise, the enormous attention surrounding Theranos and the criminal case against her had already become the subject of books, podcasts, television programs and dramatizations. Nathan Fielder's approach appears to be something considerably more unpredictable.

Nathan Fielder is best known for projects that blur the boundaries between documentary, comedy, reality television and carefully constructed situations. His television work includes Nathan for You and The Rehearsal, programs known for creating strange situations while exploring human behavior. His involvement in an Elizabeth Holmes documentary therefore immediately created curiosity about what kind of film he was making.

Fielder directed You Can See Everything with Lance Oppenheim. Oppenheim has developed a reputation for intimate documentary filmmaking, including projects that examine unusual personalities and communities. The combination of Oppenheim's documentary experience and Fielder's unconventional style makes the film particularly interesting. Instead of simply retelling the Theranos story, the filmmakers appear interested in observing Holmes herself and the complicated relationship between a subject and the people filming her.

The title You Can See Everything also fits the central idea of the project. Holmes became famous for controlling her public image during the rise of Theranos. She presented herself as a visionary entrepreneur who wanted to change medicine through technology. The eventual collapse of Theranos produced a completely different picture, with investigations revealing that the company's technology did not perform as Holmes had claimed.

Theranos had promised that a small amount of blood could be used for a wide range of medical tests. The company attracted enormous attention and investment, and Holmes became one of the most famous young entrepreneurs in America. The story eventually turned into a major corporate and criminal scandal. Holmes was convicted of fraud and sentenced to more than 11 years in prison.

What makes You Can See Everything especially unusual is the timing of the filming. Holmes apparently agreed to allow a skeptical crew into her life shortly before beginning her prison sentence. That creates a naturally tense situation. The audience knows the history of Theranos, while the filmmakers are interacting with the person at the center of that history. The result can be viewed as a study of memory, reputation, self-presentation and the difficulty of separating a person's public identity from their private explanation of events.

The first teaser has already attracted significant attention because of its uncomfortable atmosphere. Fielder questions Holmes about whether she is being genuine, creating an exchange that feels deliberately awkward. The teaser gives very little traditional documentary exposition. Instead, it relies on Holmes speaking directly to the camera and the unusual tension between her and the filmmakers.

A24 has positioned the film as a mind-bending three-year journey rather than a simple portrait. That description suggests that the project developed considerably beyond the original period immediately before Holmes entered prison. The filmmakers apparently continued working on the project over an extended period, allowing the documentary to become something larger than a straightforward final-weeks profile.

The film's secretive development has also become part of its story. Reports about Nathan Fielder meeting with Holmes had circulated before the documentary was officially announced. For a long time, audiences did not know whether the meetings were connected to a documentary, a television project or another experimental production. The mystery finally ended when You Can See Everything was revealed as the surprise A24 screening at Telluride.

The connection between Fielder and Holmes is also fascinating because both are associated with carefully controlled performances, although in completely different contexts. Fielder's work frequently examines what happens when people rehearse social situations and attempt to control unpredictable human behavior. Holmes became famous for presenting a highly controlled image of herself and Theranos. Bringing those two worlds together creates an unusual documentary setup.

The movie is not another fictional dramatization of the Theranos scandal. Elizabeth Holmes appears as herself, while Fielder and Oppenheim document her. That distinction is important for viewers who may already know the story from The Dropout or other productions. You Can See Everything is instead an observational and experimental documentary built around direct access to Holmes.

The film is scheduled for a theatrical release from A24 in October 2026. Its Telluride premiere was kept secret until the screening, adding another layer of mystery to a project that had already been rumored for a considerable period. The theatrical release should give audiences a chance to see whether the full movie is as unusual and unsettling as the teaser suggests.

For people interested in Elizabeth Holmes, Theranos, Nathan Fielder or unconventional documentaries, You Can See Everything is one of the most intriguing new films of the year. It combines a well-known real-world scandal with a filmmaker whose work frequently questions what is real, what is performed and how people behave when cameras are watching.

The biggest question surrounding the movie may not simply be what Elizabeth Holmes says. It may be how viewers interpret everything happening around her. A documentary about someone who spent years controlling a public narrative naturally raises questions about truth, performance and perception. With Fielder and Oppenheim behind the camera, those questions become even more complicated.

You Can See Everything therefore arrives with an unusual combination of secrecy, real-life controversy and experimental filmmaking. The story of Theranos is already familiar to millions of people, but this documentary offers access to a different chapter. Instead of looking backward exclusively through news reports and courtroom evidence, the film appears to ask what happens when Elizabeth Holmes is given a camera and an audience once again.

With A24 distributing the movie and Nathan Fielder directing alongside Lance Oppenheim, You Can See Everything is likely to attract audiences far beyond traditional documentary fans. It is connected to one of Silicon Valley's most famous scandals, but its real fascination may be the strange encounter between an unpredictable filmmaker and one of the most scrutinized entrepreneurs of the modern era.

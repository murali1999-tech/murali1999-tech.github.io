GBU-43/B MOAB: Why the US “Mother of All Bombs” Is in the News

Read more world news updates

The GBU-43/B MOAB, widely known as the “Mother of All Bombs,” is back in the headlines after a report on October 15, 2026, said the US military is considering whether the powerful conventional weapon could be used in potential future strikes against Iran. The report, attributed to CNN and cited by Reuters, said possible targets could include Iranian energy infrastructure and nuclear-related sites. However, this is a report about military planning, not confirmation that the bomb has been authorised for use or that an attack involving it has taken place.

Reuters said it had not independently verified the CNN report. The distinction is important because military options can be discussed without becoming an official decision. As of the report's publication, there was no confirmation in that report that the US president had approved a MOAB strike. The situation could change, but readers should not confuse consideration of a possible military option with an order to carry it out.

MOAB stands for Massive Ordnance Air Blast. Its official designation is GBU-43/B. The weapon is one of the largest conventional bombs developed for the US military and is often described as the most powerful non-nuclear weapon in the American arsenal. Its nickname sounds dramatic, but the weapon is not nuclear. It uses conventional explosives and creates a powerful blast when detonated.

The bomb was developed by the US Air Force Research Laboratory in the early 2000s. It weighs approximately 21,600 pounds, or close to 9,800 kilograms, and is around 30 feet long. Its size means it cannot be handled like a typical smaller aircraft bomb. The weapon was designed to be released from a suitable military transport aircraft and guided toward its target using satellite-based navigation and other guidance systems.

The MOAB was developed for use against large-area targets, including exposed military positions and complex terrain such as tunnel or cave networks. Its enormous blast can damage structures and equipment over a wide area. That capability is also why its possible use raises serious questions about the effects on nearby communities, civilian infrastructure and personnel who may be in or around a target area.

The weapon first became widely known in 2003, when the US military conducted its testing programme. It was designed during a period when American military planners were seeking weapons capable of producing a large conventional blast against certain battlefield targets. Although its name became famous, it was not used in combat immediately after development.

The GBU-43/B was first used operationally on April 13, 2017, in Afghanistan. US forces dropped the bomb on an Islamic State group tunnel complex in Nangarhar province. The US military said the strike was intended to destroy the group's facilities and help forces operating in the area. The attack drew international attention because it was the first confirmed combat use of the weapon.

That 2017 strike is an important part of the bomb's history, but it does not mean that the weapon is routinely used in every major military operation. It is a specialised weapon, and its size, delivery requirements and wide-area effects make decisions about its use significant. Military planners must weigh the intended target, the surrounding environment, operational risks and the wider consequences of an attack.

The latest discussion involving Iran is attracting attention because of the country's geography and the nature of some of its strategic facilities. Some sites associated with energy production and nuclear activities are protected, dispersed or located in complex terrain. But the MOAB is not the same as a specialised deep-penetrating bomb designed to attack hardened facilities buried far underground. The GBU-43/B is primarily known for its large blast effect, while the US also has other weapons designed for different target types.

It is therefore inaccurate to assume that the MOAB can destroy every underground nuclear facility. The effect of any weapon depends on the target's construction, depth, location and protective features. Public discussion of possible military options does not establish what would happen in a particular strike, and the specific details of any future operation may not be publicly available.

Another issue is the risk of escalation. A strike against major infrastructure could affect more than a military target. Energy facilities can be connected to electricity generation, industrial production and essential services. Damage may have consequences for civilians and the wider economy. If a military action causes substantial casualties or destruction, it could also influence diplomatic relations and trigger further retaliation.

The MOAB should not be confused with a nuclear bomb. Nuclear weapons release energy through nuclear reactions and can produce devastating blast, heat and radiation effects. The GBU-43/B is a conventional explosive weapon. Although its blast is extremely powerful compared with ordinary munitions, its effects and destructive mechanism are different from those of a nuclear weapon.

The phrase “Mother of All Bombs” is widely used by news outlets because it is memorable and reflects the weapon's size. The official name, Massive Ordnance Air Blast, is more descriptive of its design. Its nickname alone should not be used to judge its capabilities or predict the outcome of a military operation.

For readers following the current story, the most important questions are whether the reported option is formally approved, whether US officials make an official statement, and whether credible news organisations confirm any change in military plans. Reports based on unnamed sources can provide useful information, but they should be presented with their stated limitations. A headline suggesting that a strike is imminent may go beyond what the available reporting actually establishes.

The latest news, therefore, is not that the United States has confirmed dropping a MOAB on Iran. Rather, a report says military planners are weighing the possibility as part of potential future action. Until there is official confirmation or independently verified reporting of a decision, the status remains uncertain. The GBU-43/B's history explains why the prospect attracts attention, but its actual future use will depend on decisions that have not been confirmed in the cited report.

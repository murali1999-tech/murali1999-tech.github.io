El Niño–Southern Oscillation: What ENSO Means and How It Affects Weather

El Niño–Southern Oscillation, commonly called ENSO, is one of the most important natural climate patterns on Earth. It describes a recurring interaction between the tropical Pacific Ocean and the atmosphere above it. Although ENSO begins in the tropical Pacific, its influence can reach much farther, affecting rainfall, temperatures, storms and seasonal weather patterns in many parts of the world.

ENSO has three phases: El Niño, La Niña and a neutral phase. El Niño and La Niña represent opposite sides of the cycle, while neutral conditions occur when neither pattern is strongly present. Scientists monitor ocean temperatures, atmospheric pressure, winds and other indicators to understand which phase is developing.

The name El Niño means “the little boy” in Spanish. Fishermen along the coast of Peru and Ecuador historically noticed warmer ocean water appearing around Christmas, and the phenomenon eventually became associated with the name El Niño. La Niña means “the little girl” and is used for the opposite, cooler phase.

During El Niño, surface waters in the central and eastern tropical Pacific become warmer than average. This warming can change the normal pattern of tropical rainfall and atmospheric circulation. Warm water shifts toward the eastern Pacific, while the usual trade winds can weaken.

The atmosphere and ocean are closely connected during ENSO events. A change in ocean temperatures can influence atmospheric pressure and winds, while changes in winds can affect the movement of warm and cool water. This feedback between the ocean and atmosphere is a major reason ENSO can develop into a large-scale climate pattern.

During a typical El Niño event, the trade winds that normally blow from east to west across the tropical Pacific become weaker. As a result, warm surface water can move eastward. This changes where clouds and heavy rainfall develop over the Pacific.

The effects do not remain over the ocean. Changes in tropical Pacific heating can influence atmospheric circulation patterns far away from the region. Depending on the strength and timing of an El Niño event, parts of North America, South America, Asia, Australia and other regions can experience unusual weather.

La Niña is generally the opposite pattern. During La Niña, sea surface temperatures in the central and eastern tropical Pacific become cooler than average. Trade winds can strengthen, pushing warm surface water toward the western Pacific and allowing colder water to rise closer to the surface in the eastern Pacific.

La Niña can also influence rainfall and temperatures around the world. Some areas may become wetter than normal, while other regions may experience drier conditions. The exact effects depend on the season, location and strength of the event.

The neutral phase does not mean that the weather is completely normal everywhere. It simply means that the tropical Pacific does not meet the conditions needed to classify the situation as El Niño or La Niña. Weather systems continue to change for many other reasons.

One important part of ENSO is the Southern Oscillation. This refers to a large-scale variation in atmospheric pressure between the western and eastern tropical Pacific. Scientists discovered that changes in atmospheric pressure were connected with changes in ocean temperatures.

Because the ocean and atmosphere influence each other, the two components are now considered together. That is why the full name is El Niño–Southern Oscillation rather than simply El Niño.

ENSO events do not occur on a fixed schedule. They typically develop every few years, but the timing can vary. Some events are stronger than others, and an event can last for several months or more. Scientists therefore continuously monitor conditions in the Pacific Ocean.

Modern forecasting systems use observations from satellites, ocean buoys, ships, weather stations and other instruments. Computer models then help scientists estimate how ocean and atmospheric conditions could develop during the coming months.

ENSO forecasting is important because many societies depend on seasonal weather conditions. Farmers need information about rainfall and temperatures when making decisions about planting and harvesting. Water managers also need to understand possible changes in precipitation and drought risk.

ENSO can also affect fishing. Changes in ocean temperature and currents can alter the distribution of nutrients and marine life. Along the western coast of South America, strong El Niño events have historically affected fisheries because warmer water can reduce the availability of nutrients that support marine ecosystems.

El Niño can also influence winter weather in parts of the United States. In some years, it is associated with wetter conditions across portions of the southern United States and warmer conditions in parts of the northern United States. These are broad tendencies rather than guarantees, because individual weather events can be affected by many other factors.

La Niña can produce a different pattern. It is often associated with wetter conditions in parts of the Pacific Northwest and northern United States, while portions of the southern United States can experience warmer and drier conditions during certain seasons.

These patterns are useful for understanding climate behavior, but ENSO does not determine every weather event. A particular winter, summer or storm can differ significantly from the typical pattern associated with an ENSO phase.

ENSO also matters outside the United States. Australia and parts of Southeast Asia can experience significant rainfall changes during El Niño and La Niña. El Niño has often been associated with increased drought risk in Australia and parts of Indonesia, while La Niña can bring wetter conditions to some of these areas.

South America is also strongly affected. El Niño can produce unusually heavy rainfall in some coastal regions of Peru and Ecuador while contributing to drier conditions elsewhere. La Niña can produce a different distribution of rainfall.

ENSO can influence tropical cyclone activity as well. The relationship is complicated because different ocean basins respond differently to changes in atmospheric circulation. El Niño can increase or decrease tropical cyclone activity depending on the region.

Scientists also study ENSO because it can interact with other climate patterns. The North Atlantic Oscillation, Indian Ocean Dipole and other atmospheric and oceanic variations can influence weather at the same time. This is one reason long-range forecasting remains challenging.

It is also important to distinguish ENSO from long-term climate change. ENSO is a natural climate pattern that has occurred for thousands of years. Climate change, however, is driven primarily by the long-term increase in greenhouse gases and produces a long-term change in global temperatures and other climate conditions.

Even so, ENSO and climate change can interact. A warmer world can influence the background conditions in which ENSO events occur, while individual El Niño and La Niña events can temporarily push global average temperatures higher or lower than the underlying long-term trend.

During a strong El Niño, global average temperatures can temporarily rise because the warm tropical Pacific releases additional heat into the atmosphere. This can contribute to unusually warm years. La Niña can have the opposite short-term influence, although it does not reverse long-term global warming.

People often search for ENSO information when unusual weather begins appearing in forecasts. Understanding the difference between an El Niño event, a La Niña event and neutral conditions can help explain why seasonal forecasts may change over time.

The most useful information comes from scientific monitoring organizations that regularly publish updates about ocean temperatures, atmospheric conditions and ENSO forecasts. Forecasts can change as new observations become available, so an early prediction should not be treated as certain.

El Niño–Southern Oscillation remains an important area of climate science because of its enormous reach. A temperature change in a relatively small region of the tropical Pacific can influence atmospheric circulation across thousands of miles.

For people around the world, ENSO can affect rainfall, drought, heat, cold, agriculture, water supplies, ecosystems and seasonal weather. Understanding ENSO does not allow scientists to predict every storm or rainfall event, but it provides valuable information about the broad conditions that may develop over coming months.

El Niño, La Niña and neutral conditions are therefore best understood as parts of a connected ocean-atmosphere system. Continued observations and improved climate models allow scientists to monitor this system and provide increasingly useful seasonal forecasts.

As new ocean and atmospheric data become available, scientists will continue watching the tropical Pacific for signs of changes in ENSO conditions. For communities that depend on predictable rainfall, water supplies and agricultural seasons, those updates can be especially important.

The August 2026 lunar eclipse is one of the most interesting sky events of the year. A partial lunar eclipse will take place on August 27–28, 2026, depending on where you live. The Moon will pass through Earth's shadow, creating a striking reddish or orange appearance during the deepest part of the eclipse. NASA lists the event as a partial lunar eclipse, not a total lunar eclipse. The eclipse will be visible from large parts of the Americas, Europe, Africa and western Asia, although the exact viewing conditions depend on local time, Moon position and weather.

For people in the United States, the eclipse occurs during the night of August 27 and the early morning of August 28. In locations where the complete event is visible, the penumbral phase begins at about 9:23 p.m. EDT on August 27. The partial phase begins at approximately 10:33 p.m. EDT, and maximum eclipse occurs at about 12:12 a.m. EDT on August 28. The partial phase then continues until around 1:52 a.m., while the complete penumbral event ends at approximately 3:01 a.m. Local eclipse times vary by location.

At maximum eclipse, about 93 percent of the Moon's diameter will be inside Earth's dark umbral shadow, making this a very deep partial lunar eclipse. Because a small portion of the Moon remains outside the umbra, it does not technically become a total lunar eclipse. However, the Moon can look very close to completely eclipsed, which is why many people are searching for terms such as blood moon 2026, blood moon lunar eclipse, full lunar eclipse and total eclipse tonight.

The reddish color happens because sunlight passes through Earth's atmosphere before reaching the Moon. Earth's atmosphere scatters much of the blue light while allowing more red and orange wavelengths to reach the lunar surface. This is the same basic reason that sunsets and sunrises often appear red or orange. During a deep lunar eclipse, the Moon can therefore appear copper, orange or dark red. The exact color depends on atmospheric conditions around Earth.

A lunar eclipse happens when the Earth moves between the Sun and the Moon and the Moon enters Earth's shadow. This can happen only around the time of a full Moon and when the Sun, Earth and Moon are sufficiently aligned. A solar eclipse is different because the Moon moves between Earth and the Sun. Lunar eclipses are safe to watch with the unaided eye, unlike solar eclipses, which require proper eye protection when viewing the Sun.

People searching for “eclipse tonight,” “lunar eclipse tonight,” “is there a lunar eclipse today,” “what time is the eclipse tonight,” or “what is happening with the Moon tonight” should check their local location because eclipse times change with time zone. In Chicago and other central U.S. locations, the eclipse occurs during the night of August 27 into August 28. In western U.S. locations such as Los Angeles and San Diego, much of the event takes place during the evening of August 27. A location-specific eclipse map is the best way to determine exactly when the Moon enters the shadow and when the eclipse reaches its peak.

In India, the August 28 lunar eclipse is not directly visible because the relevant part of the event occurs during daytime for much of the country. Astronomical calculations give the maximum eclipse at approximately 9:43 a.m. Indian Standard Time, with the partial phase occurring during the morning. This means people in India generally cannot watch the eclipse directly from the ground. Online astronomy broadcasts can provide an alternative for viewers who want to follow the event.

The August 2026 event is not the only eclipse of the year. NASA's eclipse calendar lists four eclipses in 2026: an annular solar eclipse on February 17, a total lunar eclipse on March 3, a total solar eclipse on August 12, and the partial lunar eclipse on August 28. The August 28 event is therefore the second and final lunar eclipse of 2026.

The next lunar eclipse after August 2026 will be a penumbral lunar eclipse on February 20–21, 2027. Penumbral eclipses are much more subtle because the Moon passes through the lighter outer portion of Earth's shadow. The next major total solar eclipse listed by NASA will occur on August 2, 2027, with totality visible across parts of southern Spain, North Africa, the Middle East and nearby regions.

If you want to photograph the August 2026 lunar eclipse, a smartphone can capture the Moon, but a camera with a telephoto or long lens will provide much better detail. Use a tripod, manual focus and a relatively fast shutter speed. During the red phase, experiment with exposure because the eclipsed Moon is much darker than an ordinary full Moon. Binoculars or a telescope can also make the changing shadow easier to observe.

The August 2026 eclipse is a deep partial lunar eclipse rather than a true total lunar eclipse. Even so, it should provide an impressive view for observers in areas where the Moon is above the horizon during the main phases. The best viewing conditions will come from a location with clear skies and limited artificial light. Because lunar eclipses happen at night and do not require special eye protection, they are among the easiest astronomical events for families and casual skywatchers to enjoy.

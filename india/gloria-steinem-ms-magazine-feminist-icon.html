Gloria Steinem Dies at 92: Ms. Magazine Co-Founder Remembered for Her Life and Work

Gloria Steinem, the American writer, journalist, political activist and feminist organizer who became one of the most recognizable voices of the women's movement, has died at the age of 92. Steinem died at her home in New York City on Wednesday, September 2, 2026, according to reports from her foundation. Her death was reported on September 3. A cause of death has not been disclosed.

For more than five decades, Gloria Steinem was closely associated with the fight for gender equality and women's rights. She became particularly well known through her journalism, public speaking and organizing work, and she played a central role in the creation of Ms. magazine.

Steinem was born on March 25, 1934, in Toledo, Ohio. She graduated from Smith College in 1956 and later spent two years in India on a Chester Bowles Fellowship. That experience influenced her thinking about grassroots activism and organizing, which would become important parts of her later career.

After returning to the United States, Steinem developed a career as a journalist. She wrote for a number of publications and became increasingly interested in subjects involving women's lives, discrimination and political equality. Her journalism helped bring issues affecting women into mainstream public discussion.

One of the stories that helped make her nationally known was her 1963 investigation of working as a Playboy Bunny. The article, commonly known as "A Bunny's Tale," examined the conditions faced by women working at the Playboy Club and became an important early example of the investigative reporting that would shape her public reputation.

Steinem's political activism grew during the late 1960s and early 1970s. She became involved with the emerging women's movement and helped organize campaigns focused on equality, reproductive rights, political participation and social justice.

In 1971, Steinem joined with other activists to help establish the National Women's Political Caucus. The organization was created to increase women's participation in political life and encourage women to seek elected and appointed office.

In 1972, Steinem became one of the co-founders of Ms. magazine. The publication quickly became an important platform for feminist journalism and commentary. Ms. addressed subjects that were often ignored or treated as private matters by mainstream media, including workplace discrimination, reproductive rights, domestic violence and women's political representation.

Ms. magazine became closely identified with Steinem's public image, although its history involved many writers, editors, activists and supporters. Steinem served as an editor for many years and continued to be associated with the magazine as a consulting editor. The publication remains active today and continues to describe itself as a source of feminist news and analysis.

The magazine was significant because it gave women a national platform to discuss subjects that were often missing from conventional publications. Its early issues became part of the history of second-wave feminism in the United States, while later editions continued to cover politics, culture, equality and women's rights.

Steinem also became involved in numerous organizations outside Ms. She helped found the Women's Action Alliance, the Ms. Foundation for Women, the National Women's Political Caucus and the Women's Media Center. Her work also extended internationally through organizations focused on women's rights and equality.

Her activism was not limited to one issue. Throughout her career, she spoke about reproductive freedom, voting and political participation, economic equality, violence against women, racism, gender stereotypes and the representation of women in the media.

Steinem was also a prolific author. Her books included "Revolution from Within," "Moving Beyond Words," "My Life on the Road" and "The Truth Will Set You Free, But First It Will Piss You Off!" Her writing combined personal experiences with political and social observations.

Her memoir "My Life on the Road" reflected her unusual lifestyle as an organizer and speaker. Steinem spent much of her life traveling to colleges, community centers, rallies, conferences and political events. Speaking directly to people was an important part of her approach to activism.

Steinem also received many major honors during her lifetime. In 2013, President Barack Obama awarded her the Presidential Medal of Freedom, the highest civilian honor in the United States. She was also inducted into the National Women's Hall of Fame and received numerous journalism and human-rights awards.

Her influence reached well beyond the United States. She became a recognizable international figure associated with the women's rights movement and was frequently invited to speak about equality, democracy and social change.

Steinem's personal life also attracted public attention, partly because she chose to marry relatively late in life. She married David Bale in 2000. The marriage ended with his death in 2003. Steinem did not have children.

In later years, Steinem continued to write, speak and support younger activists. She remained connected to Ms. magazine and continued to participate in conversations about feminism and equality even as the movement changed with new generations.

Ms. magazine has also continued to preserve Steinem's connection to its history. The publication has published numerous articles about her career, including reflections from writers and colleagues who describe the influence she had on journalism and feminist activism.

Her death marks the end of the life of one of the most prominent American feminist figures of the modern era. While debates surrounding feminism have changed considerably since the 1960s and 1970s, many of the subjects Steinem helped bring into public conversation remain part of political and social debate.

For readers searching for Gloria Steinem, Gloria, Ms. magazine or information about Gloria Steinem's death, the central part of her legacy is the combination of journalism, activism and organizing. She used all three to push women's issues into national conversations and helped create institutions that continued working after her individual campaigns ended.

Ms. magazine remains one of the clearest parts of that legacy. The publication that began in 1972 continues to publish feminist reporting and analysis in print and online, connecting its current work with the movement that helped establish it more than five decades ago.

Gloria Steinem's career therefore cannot be separated from the broader history of women's rights in the United States. Her work as a journalist brought attention to women's experiences, her activism helped build political organizations, and Ms. provided a lasting media platform for feminist ideas.

As news of her death spreads, tributes are expected from journalists, activists, political leaders and organizations that were influenced by her work. Her life and career will remain a major part of the history of American journalism and the women's movement.
